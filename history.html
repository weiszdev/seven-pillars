History & bias: how medical evidence gets made

Before reading any trial result, positive or negative, it helps to know who paid for the research, who designed it, and which questions never got studied at all. This page lays that out. It documents bias where the record shows bias, and it marks contested claims as contested.

1. The Flexner Report (1910) and the remaking of American medicine

  • In 1910 Abraham Flexner published Medical Education in the United States and Canada for the Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching. He recommended closing most of the roughly 155 North American medical schools and rebuilding the rest on a university, laboratory-science model (Johns Hopkins). PubMed 21966046
  • Philanthropy put money behind the report. Rockefeller's General Education Board and the Carnegie philanthropies gave large grants to the schools that fit the new model. Schools that could not meet the standards, including most homeopathic and eclectic schools, closed or merged. PubMed 21966046
  • The report also hurt Black medical education. Of the seven Black medical schools of the time, Flexner recommended that only Howard and Meharry stay open. The others closed, and a 2020 analysis estimated that tens of thousands of African American physicians who might otherwise have trained were lost by 2019. PubMed 32816033 · PubMed 36512812 · PubMed 33818380
  • The result was a lasting tilt toward lab science, drugs, and surgery, and away from nutrition, lifestyle, and traditional practice. This shaped what medical schools teach and what researchers study.
  • Contested Some writers argue that Rockefeller interests set out on purpose to suppress natural medicine so they could sell petroleum-based drugs. The documented record supports the funding, the standardization, and the closures. I could not find primary documents proving that deliberate motive, so this page treats it as an open claim, not an established fact.

1a. Timeline: from many schools of medicine to one

  • Before 1910: About 155 medical schools in North America. Many were small for-profit schools. They taught a mix of approaches (regular/allopathic, homeopathic, eclectic or botanical), and standards varied widely. PubMed 21966046
  • 1910: Flexner's report for the Carnegie Foundation. It called for a few university-based, lab-science schools modeled on Johns Hopkins.
  • 1910s–1930s: Rockefeller's General Education Board and Carnegie money funded the schools that fit the model. State licensing boards raised their standards. Most proprietary, homeopathic, and eclectic schools closed or merged, and the number of schools fell by more than half by the 1930s. PubMed 21966046 · PubMed 15126445
  • 1930s–1990s: Medical training centered on pharmacology, surgery, and lab science. Nutrition and lifestyle got little teaching time.
  • 1990s–today: The internet gives patients direct access to research papers, practitioner books, and patient networks. Some of it is excellent and some is unreliable, which is why this site grades evidence.

Black medical schools: a deeper loss

Seven Black medical schools existed in 1910. Flexner recommended keeping only Howard and Meharry, and the other five (Flint, Leonard, Louisville National, Knoxville, and the University of West Tennessee) closed by 1923. In Chapter XIV, Flexner wrote: The practice of the negro doctor will be limited to his own race, which in its turn will be cared for better by good negro physicians than by poor white ones. He also wrote that Black Americans needed medical education partly because of their tremendous importance that belongs to a potential source of infection and contagion (Flexner Report, p. 180, Wikisource scan). Campbell et al. (JAMA Netw Open 2020) estimated that if those five schools had stayed open, they could have trained an additional 27,773 to 35,315 graduates by 2019. Black physicians remain underrepresented today. PubMed 32816033

NotDrWise perspective

Perspective: this is a viewpoint, not a documented finding

As we see it, Flexner-era standardization pushed natural healing knowledge out of mainstream medical culture for about 70 years. Doctors were left with essentially one toolkit, drugs and surgery, closely tied to pharmaceutical relationships. Now that anyone can reach this knowledge online, it can no longer be suppressed.

Documented facts that support this view: the school closures and funding described above; the AMA's illegal boycott of chiropractic (Wilk, below); and measurable industry sponsorship bias (section 2).

Contested The claim that the knowledge was intentionally eliminated, and the "70 years" figure, are interpretations. Historians also credit the reforms with removing schools that were unsafe or diploma mills. Some natural approaches survived outside medical schools, in naturopathy, chiropractic, and herbalism.

1b. Spears Chiropractic Hospital, Denver (1943–1984)

Leo L. Spears, DC, a 1921 graduate of the Palmer School, opened the hospital's first building (236 beds) on May 1, 1943. A second building (364 beds) opened in 1949, for about 600 beds in total. Historian Joseph Keating described it as the largest inpatient facility in chiropractic history. Patients came with chronic and "hopeless" conditions such as paralysis, multiple sclerosis, polio, and cancer. The hospital closed in March 1984, and the buildings were demolished in 1993. (Keating, PubMed 11613388; Dynamic Chiropractic; profile with court history)

Experiential / case-based: no comparison groups The hospital's own literature reported "gratifying results," including with multiple sclerosis patients (Spears promotional materials, archived). I found no published outcome figures from an independent source, so this page gives none.

Conflicts with medical organizations: In 1946 Colorado revoked the hospital's license. In 1950 the Colorado Supreme Court reversed, ruling the license conditions void (Spears Free Clinic & Hospital v. State Board of Health, 122 Colo. 147). In 1952 the Tenth Circuit dismissed Spears's antitrust suit against the Denver Medical Society on jurisdiction grounds, not on its merits. The AMA's Department of Investigation kept a file on Spears from 1926 to 1974 (AMA records, SNAC).

1c. Wilk v. American Medical Association (1987; affirmed 1990)

In 1987 Judge Susan Getzendanner of the Northern District of Illinois (671 F. Supp. 1465) ruled that the AMA had violated the Sherman Act by running a boycott of chiropractors. The AMA did this through its 1963 Committee on Quackery and an ethics rule that made it unethical for medical doctors to associate with "unscientific practitioners." Explaining why she issued an injunction, she wrote of the systematic, long-term wrongdoing and the long-term intent to destroy a licensed profession. The Seventh Circuit affirmed in 1990: the AMA ... violated § 1 of the Sherman Act ... by conducting an illegal boycott in restraint of trade directed at chiropractors generally (895 F.2d 352; text; NYT 1987).

What the case did not decide: The court found the boycott ended in 1980. It did not rule on whether chiropractic treatment works for any condition.

1d. Harry Hoxsey and the Hoxsey herbal treatment

Clinics: Hoxsey, who had no medical training, opened a cancer clinic in Taylorville, Illinois, in 1924. He opened one in Dallas on March 9, 1936. By the 1950s he had clinics in several states, and the Dallas clinic was among the largest private cancer clinics in the country (Handbook of Texas).

Libel suit against Fishbein and Hearst papers: Hoxsey sued over an article called "Medical Hucksters" that labeled him a "quack." On March 18, 1949, the federal court in Dallas found that the claim that he had "hood-winked" jurists was untrue. It awarded him nominal damages of $1, plus $1 for the slander of his father. The court reasoned that the controversy had not reduced his income. It also noted that Fishbein, the AMA, and Hearst were dismissed for lack of service (Hoxsey v. Fishbein, 83 F. Supp. 282; opinion). Contested Supporters count this as a win. The court treated it as a token one.

The 1953 Fitzgerald report: Benedict Fitzgerald, special counsel to a U.S. Senate committee, wrote a memorandum alleging that organized medicine had conspired to suppress Hoxsey and other cancer treatments. Congressional staff report, not a court or committee finding It was entered in the Congressional Record, but it never became an official conclusion of the committee (document summary).

FDA and court actions: In United States v. Hoxsey Cancer Clinic (5th Cir. 1952, 198 F.2d 273), the appeals court reversed a trial court that had sided with Hoxsey. It found the labeling claims false and misleading and ordered an injunction against interstate shipment. The court gave patient testimonials "no weight" without biopsy confirmation (opinion). In 1956 the FDA posted public warnings. The U.S. clinics closed over the next few years, and the Dallas clinic shut in 1960.

Tijuana: In 1963 Mildred Nelson, Hoxsey's longtime nurse, opened the Bio-Medical Center in Tijuana, Mexico. She first worked for Hoxsey after her mother was treated there in 1946. She ran the center until her death in 1999 and claimed high cure rates (HerbalGram obituary).

Experiential / case-based Evidence status: No controlled human trial of the Hoxsey formula has ever been published. Some of its herbs have lab-level anticancer activity. The 1990 U.S. Office of Technology Assessment report Unconventional Cancer Treatments reviewed Hoxsey and did not find it effective. It also noted that the treatment had never been properly evaluated. The clinic's cure-rate claims and the critics' "no verified cures" verdict have never been tested against each other in a controlled study.

2. How money shapes the evidence today

  • Sponsorship bias is measurable. A Cochrane review of 75 studies (Lundh et al. 2017) found that drug and device trials paid for by industry reported favorable efficacy results more often (risk ratio about 1.27) and favorable conclusions more often (about 1.34) than trials with other funding. Their standard risk-of-bias scores were not worse, so the bias tends to come from design choices, such as which comparator to use, the dose, the outcomes, and what gets published. PubMed 28207928 · Earlier reviews found the same pattern: PubMed 12775614 · PubMed 12533125
  • Selective publication. Turner et al. (NEJM 2008) compared FDA files with published papers on 12 antidepressants. Almost all positive trials were published. Most negative or questionable trials either stayed unpublished or were written up as positive. In the published literature, 94% of trials looked positive. In the FDA files, 51% were positive. PubMed 18199864
  • Ghostwriting. Industry documents released in the rofecoxib (Vioxx) litigation showed that company staff or hired writers drafted manuscripts, which academic "guest authors" then signed. PubMed 18413874
  • Who sets the agenda. Large trials cost millions of dollars. Companies fund trials for products they can patent. Diet, sleep, water, fellowship, prayer, and generic nutrients have no patent holder to pay for a definitive trial, so they are studied less and often in smaller, shorter, observational studies. When a pillar has no RCT, that usually means no one has paid for one. It does not mean the pillar was tested and failed.
  • Which way the bias runs. Documented sponsorship bias usually favors the sponsor's own product. It is better evidence that drug benefits get overstated than that trials of unpatentable approaches get rigged to fail. Each negative trial still has to be judged on its own design.

3. The Gonzalez pancreatic trial: both sides

The published result found no benefit for the enzyme regimen. Read the design critiques below with it.

What the conventional evidence says (Chabot 2010 results)

What was published. In 1998 the NCI (with NCCAM) sponsored a randomized trial of the Gonzalez enzyme regimen against gemcitabine chemotherapy for inoperable pancreatic cancer. Most eligible patients refused randomization, so in 2001 the trial became an observational study in which patients chose their own arm. Of 55 patients, 23 chose chemotherapy and 32 chose enzymes. Median survival was 14 months with chemotherapy and 4.3 months with enzymes (adjusted hazard ratio 6.96), and quality of life was better in the chemotherapy group (Chabot et al., J Clin Oncol 2010). PubMed 19687327

What critics, including Dr. Gonzalez, raised. Patients chose their arm, so the study was not randomized. Some enzyme-arm patients could not eat well enough to follow the regimen. Some did not meet eligibility rules. Enrollment stopped before the planned minimum of 72 patients. Gonzalez was not an author and did not see the manuscript before publication. He laid out his case in What Went Wrong: The Truth Behind the Clinical Trial of the Enzyme Treatment of Cancer (New Spring Press, 2012).

What oversight bodies found. In 2008 the federal Office for Human Research Protections (OHRP) found that consent was not properly documented before research activities began for 40 of 62 people considered for the trial, and that two patients had been enrolled outside the 8-week window. It found that consent was obtained before any treatment, and it found no violation in the early stop. HHS concluded there was not material evidence that the findings were invalid. (HHS/ASPE response; OHRP letter of June 2, 2008, as reproduced)

A fair reading. This was not a clean randomized test. Both selection and adherence could have biased it. It also produced no sign of benefit, and the survival gap was large. The question of whether the full regimen, followed faithfully by eligible patients, would do better has never been answered by a well-run randomized trial.

4. What RCTs can and cannot do

Real limits for whole-lifestyle, individualized protocols:

  • Blinding is often impossible. People know whether they changed their diet or started praying.
  • A whole-life protocol is hard to stick to, and in a trial the effect of "assigned" is not the same as the effect of "actually done."
  • Trials often enroll very sick, late-stage patients who have exhausted other options. The MISTRAL mistletoe trial in advanced pancreatic cancer is one example.
  • Follow-up is often too short to show effects on prevention or recurrence.
  • A standardized protocol can strip out the individualization that practitioners say matters.

What RCTs still do well:

  • They protect against the placebo effect and wishful thinking.
  • Randomization balances hidden differences between groups, such as healthier, wealthier, or more motivated people choosing a treatment.
  • They guard against survivor bias. Remission stories come from people who lived, so they cannot tell us how many people followed the same path and did not survive.

The honest position: an RCT is a tool, and like any tool it can be well or badly designed, and well or badly funded. On this site we show every result, positive and negative, together with the context needed to judge it.
